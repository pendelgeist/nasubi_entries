# Nasubi In Korea: The Sequel Nobody Asked For

## The Setup

After Nasubi (なすび) finally reached 1,000,000 JPY and was released from his apartment on *Susunu! Denpa Shonen*, the
producers pulled one of the cruelest twists in television history: they immediately transported him — still naked — to
South Korea and told him to do it all over again. This time the goal was 1,000,000 KRW (Korean Won), roughly 100,000 JPY
or ~$770 USD at the time.

He was placed in another empty apartment. He didn't speak Korean. He couldn't read the magazines. He had no idea what
most of the contests were even for. And yet he filled out postcards.

pork belly, beef brisket, steak, and whole chicken. Korea's sweepstakes scene clearly leaned into the country's BBQ
culture. This is a stark contrast to Japan, where meat was a relatively small category.

## Japan vs. Korea: A Comparison

| Dimension            | Japan                    | Korea                   |
|----------------------|--------------------------|-------------------------|
| Duration             | ~11 months               | ~3 months               |
| Total entries        | 1,891                    | 358                     |
| Goal                 | 1,000,000 JPY            | 1,000,000 KRW           |
| Goal in USD          | ~$7,700                  | ~$770                   |
| Food % of entries    | 51%                      | 36.3%                   |
| Entry counts tracked | Yes (60,911 postcards)   | No (all marked "x")     |
| Language barrier     | None                     | Severe                  |

The Korea challenge was fundamentally different in scale. The goal was roughly **one-tenth** the value of the Japanese
target. The time frame was compressed. But the psychological cruelty was arguably greater — Nasubi had just finished
an 11-month ordeal, believed he was free, and was immediately thrust into the same nightmare in a country where he
couldn't even read.

## The Korean Oddities

The Korea entries reveal some uniquely Korean contest prizes, as well as some entries that highlight Nasubi's
bewilderment at Korean magazine culture:

- **"Probably beef (100g)"** (たぶん牛肉) — He literally couldn't read what the contest was for
- **"Probably a Polaroid camera"** (おそらくポラロイドカメラ) — Best guess from the pictures
- **"Apparently a TV"** (どう見てもテレビ) — "No matter how you look at it, it's a TV"
- **"Probably an electronic organizer"** (たぶん電子手帳) — Guessing from the product photo
- **"Probably a pager"** (たぶんポケベル) — 1999 technology identification challenge
- **"Something like a cross between a mini 4WD and a radio-controlled car"** (ミニ四駆とラジコンの中間みたいな感じの物)
- **"Spread it on bread and eat it. Butter-like substance (bulgogi flavor)"** — This one speaks for itself
- **"Filthy trip to Mount Kumgang"** (汚い金剛山の旅) — An... unfortunate translation of a North Korean mountain resort trip
- **"Traditional Korean classical musical instrument"** (韓国伝統の古典楽器) — What would a naked man in an empty apartment
  do with a gayageum?
- **"3cm nose without plastic surgery"** (整形手術なしの3cm鼻) — A cosmetic device of unclear purpose
- **"Voice recognition doll 'Sore (or Heta)-chan'"** — Even the transcriber wasn't sure of the name

The "probably" and "apparently" entries are heartbreaking. In Japan, Nasubi could at least read the contest rules and
know what he was entering. In Korea, he was filling out postcards for prizes he could only identify from packaging
photos.

## Appliances: Building a Home He'd Never Live In

Nasubi entered 34 appliance contests — refrigerators (冷蔵庫), washing machines (洗濯機), microwaves, pressure cookers,
rice cookers (電子ジャー), vacuum cleaners, humidifiers, toasters, and even an air purifier. Multiple entries for the same
appliance type appear across both months.

This mirrors his Japanese experience, where he won a desk and chair he didn't need and a VCR he couldn't use. The
absurdity of a naked man in an empty room entering contests for a fully automatic washing machine (全自動洗濯機) needs no
commentary.

## Key Differences from Japan

1. **The language barrier changed everything.** In Japan, Nasubi's problem was odds. In Korea, his problem was
   comprehension. The "probably" entries show he was gambling blind.

2. **Food strategy shifted toward meat.** In Japan, he spread entries across rice, noodles, snacks, and produce. In
   Korea, he went hard on BBQ meat — possibly because it was the one thing he could clearly identify in Korean
   magazines.

3. **The goal was 10x easier** — 1,000,000 KRW vs 1,000,000 JPY — but the barriers were higher. A lower target in a
   foreign language may have been equally or more difficult in practice.

4. **No postcard counts survive.** We can't analyze volume strategy, ROI, or brute-force patterns the way we can with
   the Japanese data. The Korea segment exists as a catalog of what he tried, not how hard he tried.

5. **The cruelty was the point.** The Japan challenge could be framed as a quirky endurance test. The Korea sequel —
   sprung on an exhausted, psychologically broken man who thought he was finally free — was pure exploitation.
